The sequence of events
The BoJ lifted the policy target rate from 0%-0.10% to 0.25% in a 7-2 vote and reduced the size of its monthly gross
Japanese government bond (JGB) purchases from the current ~¥6.0trn to ¥5.31trn for August and September at its July
31st meeting. The BoJ also signaled the size of its monthly bond purchases will be further reduced by ¥400bn each
quarter thereafter until January-March 2026 to reach ¥2.9trn (a 50% cut to its monthly bond purchases).
But the move lower in USDJPY (Yen strengthening) and its impact on Japanese stock markets had already begun
starting July 11th following a suspected BoJ intervention that saw USDJPY drop 2.5% from it’s near all-time highs
~161.80. But the drop did not end there, instead, USDJPY continued its decline by a further 6 -7% over the next two
weeks leading up to the BoJ’s July 31st meeting when Japan government minister Kono suggested the BoJ needs to
hike rates to lift JPY which is "too cheap" (a rare intervention in BoJ’s affairs by the government) and senior Liberal
Democratic Party (LDP) official Motegi told Nikkei news that the BoJ should clarify its normalization path. Investors
sensed a rise in tensions between the BoJ and Japanese government and in the couple of days just prior to the BoJ
meeting, press reports circulated that the BoJ was indeed, weighing a rate hike as well as a cut to its bond purchases.
The BoJ’s decision to lift rates and cut bond purchases therefore, looked more an opportunity to catch-up to an
extended period of elevated headline inflation and address the government’s ire about a weak Yen at the same time.
Still, the BoJ’s forward guidance leaned slightly hawkish when it stated – “….given that real interest rates are at
significantly low levels, if the outlook for economic activity and prices presented in the July Outlook Report will be
realized, the Bank will accordingly continue to raise the policy interest rate and adjust the degree of monetary
accommodation”.
Then came the Fed FOMC meeting where the most significant change to the statement was to signal that the
Committee is now “attentive to the risks on both sides of its dual mandate”. Chair Powell indicated that while inflation
is running “somewhat” above target, upside risks are now less prevalent, on the other hand, he saw building downside
risk to the labor market. He also pointed to the unemployment rate as a useful summary statistic to watch and made
multiple references to whether the rise in the unemployment rate was something more than “normalization”. His final
comment that there was a “real conversation” within the FOMC about the potential to begin rate cuts immediately”
was unexpected. Then came the US July jobs report that saw the unemployment rate at 4.25% and very close to the
4.28% level that would have triggered the “Sahm rule”.



Market price action
Since July 11th, the yen has strengthened against USD to a 141.70 low (from a 161.85 high) representing a 12.5% drop
which has taken other low yielding funding currencies (CHF and CNH) with it (FIGURE 1), both strengthening 5.6% and
2.3% respectively as the high FX volatility and strengthening yen has led to a rapid unwinding of carry trades across the
board. Stock markets have also tumbled with the Nikkei down 26% from its July 11th highs, underperforming both the
S&P 500 and Nasdaq that have fallen 8.5% and 15.7% respectively during the same period, indicating the stronger yen
has contributed to the Nikkei’s decline. Rates markets too have been quick to reprice the Fed to deliver 125bp of cuts
this year (versus 75bp previously) and 250bp in cuts to the end of 2025 (from 200bp previously). Fed pricing has
moved to the dovish end of the spectrum versus other major central bank pricing which has led to DXY declining by
~2.5% since July 11. Markets have also repriced the BoJ, taking the 10-15bp hike priced earlier for October, off the table
(FIGURE 2).

Where to now for the Yen?
By all measures, in our view, the selloff in USDJPY and its subsequent impact on Japanese stocks looks overdone. It is
estimated that the Fed Funds to BoJ overnight rate spread would need to compress to ~350bp from the current 500-
525bp (a 200bp yield compression) to drive USDJPY lower to the mid 130s. Even if the Fed and BoJ were to validate
current market pricing (Fed lowers rates by 125bp to year-end and the BoJ keeps the overnight rate unchanged at
0.25%), that could only represent a little over half the yield compression likely required to drive USDJPY further lower to
the mid 130s.
The OCIS base case however is that the Fed cuts 75bp this year and sees current market pricing as rather overdone.
This is because historically, there’s been a routine pattern of downside economic surprises in the summer months
which makes it unlikely that a single jobs report could be heralding the beginning of some great negative turning point
as implied by the current volatility. Positioning wise, carry trades across G10 have now also been unwound dramatically
by leveraged players which seems to be reflected in the recent culling of yen shorts by speculative accounts (FIGURE 3).
But, there are other groups of investors who may still be looking to unwind yen shorts. These include –
Offshore buyers of Japanese stocks FX hedged who have been hurt by the sharp fall in stock prices and may have
begun to lift their forward FX hedges through buying JPY/ selling USD forward in ana attempt to take advantage of a
strengthening Yen to compensate for stock losses. They may still have more to do.
Japanese exporters too may be worried as current USDJPY levels are now well below their USDJPY break evens (
~144.33 according to the latest BoJ Tankan survey) and may have started to hedge their forward earnings. They may
also have more to do.
Repatriation by Japanese pension funds and life insurers into domestic bonds from USTs and USD denominated
corporate bonds while simultaneously unwinding the FX staretgy – they will likely need US Treasuries to trade more
expensively to domestic Japanese bonds (JGBs) at spreads sub +250bp in 10Yrs from the current +295bp.
With 4 investor groups (including leveraged players) either in the process of unwinding or looking to commence
unwinding their yen short positioning, and Japanese policymakers (BoJ and the MoF) now more aligned to cap yen
weakness, it is difficult to see USDJPY getting past and sustaining levels above 150.00 even if the Fed rate cut path
proves to be less aggressive than what is currently priced.

Bonds are affected by a number of risks, including fluctuations in interest rates, credit risk and prepayment risk. In general, as prevailing interest
rates rise, fixed income securities prices will fall. Bonds face credit risk if a decline in an issuer’s credit rating, or creditworthiness, causes a bond’s
price to decline. High yield bonds are subject to additional risks such as increased risk of default and greater volatility because of the lower credit
quality of the issues. Finally, bonds can be subject to prepayment risk. When interest rates fall, an issuer may choose to borrow money at a lower
interest rate, while paying off its previously issued bonds. As a consequence, underlying bonds will lose the interest payments from the investment
and will be forced to reinvest in a market where prevailing interest rates are lower than when the initial investment was made.
